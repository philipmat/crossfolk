# How crossword generation works

Crossfolk does not ask an AI to draw a crossword grid. The AI, when it is needed, supplies possible themed answers and clues. The browser then builds and checks the grid locally. Built-in categories do not need the AI because their vocabulary is shipped with the app.

The main generation policy is in `public/engine.js`. The grid solver is in `public/dense.js`.

## What “crossed” means

A playable square is **crossed**, or **checked**, when it belongs to both an Across answer and a Down answer. The crossing percentage is:

> squares used by both an Across and a Down answer / all playable squares

Blocked squares are not part of this calculation.

The target crossing percentages are:

| Difficulty | Minimum checked letters |
| --- | ---: |
| Easy | 90% |
| Medium | 80% |
| Hard | 60% |

These are minimums, not goals to stop at. The current Medium layout is fully checked, and the current Large layouts are normally around 87% to 91% checked.

The difficulty setting also selects the appropriate clue wording. On Large puzzles, Easy uses a six-letter connecting answer while Medium and Hard use a nine-letter connecting answer.

## Where the words come from

For a built-in category, `public/engine.js` combines several sources:

1. A small hand-written bank of recognizable answers with Easy, Medium, and Hard clues.
2. The explicitly curated short-answer lists in `public/theme-fill.js`.
3. Context-specific clues for ambiguous answers in `public/theme-clues.js`.
4. Explicitly curated plurals in `public/theme-plurals.js`.
5. The larger category lists in `public/wordnet-words.js`.

The WordNet file is generated by `scripts/build-wordnet.mjs`. Category words may be as long as 13 letters. The shared general-fill list is limited to nine letters to keep the browser download smaller. The WordNet builder selects category entries by their lexical group or by words in their definitions. For example, Travel includes WordNet locations plus definitions referring to cities, countries, continents, islands, mountains, rivers, deserts, landmarks, and other geographic features.

This larger vocabulary is important for two reasons. It gives the solver more possible letter combinations, and it lets Medium and Large puzzles use substantial answers instead of relying almost entirely on three- and four-letter words.

For a custom category, the server asks the configured AI service for candidate themed answers and clues. Those candidates go through the same local grid solver and the same validity rules. General dictionary answers may help connect the grid, but they are never counted as themed.

## How a pattern is filled

A pattern says which squares are open and which are blocked. The solver turns each horizontal or vertical run of at least three open squares into an answer slot.

For each slot, the solver first finds all unused words of the correct length. It narrows that list whenever a crossing fixes a letter. If a possible answer for one slot cannot agree with any answer in a crossing slot, it is removed immediately.

The solver repeatedly chooses the unfinished slot with the fewest remaining choices. It tries a word, carries its letters into the crossing slots, and continues. If that choice makes another slot impossible, it removes the word and tries the next one. This is ordinary backtracking, with early elimination of impossible choices so it does not explore obviously bad branches.

Themed answers are preferred, and some slots are reserved for them before the deeper search begins. A finished grid is accepted only when a strict majority of its answers are themed. The same answer cannot appear twice.

Generation history is also considered. Previously used answers are tried later, and a puzzle is rejected if its complete answer set already appears in the supplied history. Individual words may return in later games; an entire answer set may not.

Normal play uses random ordering among otherwise similar choices. Tests can inject a seeded random-number function so the regression cases are repeatable.

## Why Medium and Large use modules

Trying to fill one large, tightly crossed 13 × 13 pattern in a single search creates too many interacting choices. Instead, Crossfolk solves several small, dense regions and then joins them with validated answers.

### Small, 5 × 5

Small puzzles use the dense mini patterns directly. The important product rule is at least 90% checked letters and at least 19 playable squares. A hard Small puzzle is not silently allowed to become sparser when the solver has trouble.

### Medium, 9 × 9

The Medium constructor solves two fully checked mini regions. Each region has one extended answer, and the regions are joined through a shared, preselected letter. The final result is connected and currently has 100% checked letters. It also contains an answer of at least six letters.

### Large, 13 × 13

The Large constructor solves three dense mini regions independently. It then finds one horizontal answer that connects the two upper regions and one vertical answer that connects the upper bridge to the lower region.

Easy uses a six-letter horizontal bridge. Medium and Hard use a nine-letter bridge. These bridge lengths guarantee that Large puzzles contain longer answers. Most module squares are crossed in both directions; a few bridge squares have only one clue, which is why the completed layout is usually about 87% to 91% checked rather than 100%.

If a selected set of modules has no suitable bridge answers, it is discarded and another combination is tried. The completed puzzle is checked again for a strict themed majority, duplicate answers, and prior use of the same answer set.

The normal dense-search budget is 2.5 seconds for Small and five seconds for Medium or Large. Older whole-grid and greedy construction paths remain as fallbacks. Regardless of which path returns a puzzle, the regression tests require the final result to meet the same crossing and validity rules.

## Rules every result must preserve

The generator and its tests require all of the following:

- Every answer stays inside the grid.
- Letters agree wherever answers cross.
- Adjacent letters belong to the same Across or Down answer; accidental letter runs are invalid.
- Every answer is connected to the rest of the puzzle through crossings.
- No answer is duplicated within a puzzle.
- A strict majority of answers are genuinely related to the category.
- Answer numbering follows normal reading order.
- A previously supplied complete answer set is not reused.
- The crossing and playable-area requirements for the selected size and difficulty are met.

## Tests that cover generation

Run the complete suite with:

```sh
npm test
```

The generation checks are mainly in `test/engine.test.js`:

- `assertValidPuzzle` checks dimensions, answer placement, crossing letters, connectivity, numbering, and accidental adjacent runs.
- `hard minis combine at least 90% crossing coverage with a thematic majority` runs every built-in category through the Small/Hard contract.
- `repeated hard minis keep full interlocking and distinct answer sets` checks history handling and repeat generation.
- `every built-in theme meets the large-grid crossing target at each difficulty` checks every built-in category at 13 × 13 against 90% Easy, 80% Medium, and 60% Hard. It also checks the themed majority, longer answers, and minimum board use.
- `medium grids use longer answers and meet the crossing targets for every built-in theme` checks every built-in category at 9 × 9.
- `every built-in theme has longer vocabulary for medium and large grids` requires at least 20 category answers of six or more letters and at least 10 of eight or more letters.
- `travel vocabulary includes named places and geographic features` protects Travel's distinctive content.
- `a future theme with a sufficiently rich vocabulary uses the same density contract` builds a representative Architecture category directly from a large candidate pool and tests all three crossing targets. This confirms that the solver is not dependent on the names of the current categories.
- The theme-picker tests confirm that every declared built-in category appears in the interface and that saved category choices are restored correctly.

The density tests use fixed random seeds. A failure therefore represents a repeatable change in the algorithm or vocabulary rather than an unlucky shuffle.

## Adding a new built-in category

Use this procedure when introducing a category that should work without the AI service:

1. Add the category name to `supportedThemes` in `public/themes.js`.
2. Add useful aliases for it in `THEME_ALIASES` in the same file. Aliases decide when free-form text should resolve to the built-in category.
3. Add the category to the preset list in `public/index.html`.
4. Add a small, high-quality starter bank to `BANKS` in `public/engine.js`. Include Easy, Medium, and Hard clue wording for each answer.
5. Add clearly related short answers to `public/theme-fill.js`. Do not label a word as themed merely because it is convenient filler.
6. Add context-specific clues to `public/theme-clues.js` for words whose ordinary clue would not express the intended category meaning.
7. Add appropriate noun plurals to `public/theme-plurals.js` when useful.
8. Add the category key to the generated theme maps in `scripts/build-wordnet.mjs`. Give it a careful definition matcher and, where appropriate, a WordNet lexical-category rule.
9. Regenerate the dictionary with `node scripts/build-wordnet.mjs /path/to/WordNet-3.0/dict`. Review a sample of the generated answers and clues for false matches, unsuitable language, awkward abbreviations, and misleading senses. A broad regular expression can create a large but poor category, so candidate count alone is not enough.
10. Add a category-specific regression test for its defining vocabulary, similar to the Travel place-and-feature test. Include several unmistakable examples rather than only generic words.
11. Run `npm test`.

Once the new name is in `supportedThemes`, the existing Small, Medium, Large, themed-majority, long-vocabulary, layout-validity, and theme-picker loops automatically include it. The new category must therefore provide enough varied words to pass all sizes and difficulties. As a practical floor, retain at least 20 answers of six or more letters and at least 10 answers of eight or more letters, but favor semantic quality and varied letter patterns over simply reaching those counts.

If the category cannot meet the density tests, first improve the candidate bank: add accurate words across several lengths, remove near-duplicates and obscure forms, and make sure longer answers have varied letters. Change the layout or lower a product threshold only when the category exposes a genuine general limitation, not to make one thin vocabulary pass.
